Consistency checks of the definitions

Main theorems states the six results in full and links each to its independent Lean statement and definitions. This page explains how those definitions describe the mathematical objects and how they are related to the definitions used in the proofs. The identifications described below are proved in Lean.

Start with the objects occurring in the theorem you want to inspect: cubes, rectangle families, averages, overlap and maximal functions. The cross-coordinate averages and projection are constructions used in the proof and are described afterward. The independent specification guide explains how the separate statements are organized and connected to the proofs.

Cubes and grids

A dyadic grid in \(\mathbb R^{d_i}\) consists of half-open cubes. At each integer scale \(k\), the cubes have side length \(2^k\) and partition the space; any two intersecting cubes are nested. The dyadic children of a cube are obtained by halving each side. The results allow arbitrary such grids, with no assumption that every finite collection has a common ancestor.

Both descriptions require cubes of the prescribed side length to partition the space at each scale. The independent specification requires intersecting cubes to be nested; the proof library requires the usual dyadic children to belong to the next generation. Lean proves these descriptions equivalent, preserving the cubes themselves. The scale indices are related by \(n=-k\). Standard and shifted grids are constructed explicitly. Within a fixed cube, its descendants obtained by repeated halving are proved to be exactly the grid cubes of the corresponding smaller side length.

Lean: grid used in the proofs, independent grid definition, dyadic children, equivalence of the two descriptions, standard grids, shifted grids, and descendants.

Product coordinates and averages

Write \(\mathbb R^d=\mathbb R^{d_1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb R^{d_m}\), where \(d=\sum_i d_i\). A rectangle is a product \(I=I^1\times\cdots\times I^m\) of cubes. Listing the scalar coordinates in order identifies this product space with \(\mathbb R^d\). The formalization proves that this identification preserves Lebesgue measure, rectangles, integrals, averages and norms.

For a cube \(Q\) in the \(j\)th coordinate, \(E_Q\) averages in \(x_j\) over \(Q\), leaves the other coordinates unchanged, and is zero when \(x_j\notin Q\). Here \(x_j\) ranges over \(\mathbb R^{d_j}\). These coordinate averages preserve measurability. For the signed bounded measurable functions used in the finite construction, averaging in every coordinate is proved to give exactly \(E_I f=\mathbf1_I|I|^{-1}\int_I f\).

Lean: identification of the coordinates, preservation of Lebesgue measure, rectangle, integral and norm identities, rectangles in the independent statement, coordinate averages, and averaging over a rectangle.

Families, overlap and maximal functions

A family is weakly incomparable if there are no members \(I,J\) with \(I^i\subsetneq J^i\) for every \(i\). Equivalently, a containment \(I\subset J\) leaves at least one cube factor unchanged: \(I^i=J^i\) for some \(i\). This equivalence is proved in Lean. Ordinary incomparability means that no distinct members contain one another and implies weak incomparability. Sparseness means that there are pairwise disjoint measurable sets \(E_I\subset I\) with \(|E_I|\geq\eta|I|\). These conditions are stated directly in terms of the rectangles and their subsets; membership in the dyadic grids is a separate hypothesis.

The independent statements allow Lebesgue-measurable sets \(E_I\). Each has a Borel subset of the same measure, so replacing these sets preserves containment, disjointness and the exact value of \(\eta\). This proves the equivalence with the formulation using Borel sets in the proofs. The equivalence and its use in the stated results are formalized.

For a rectangle family \(\mathcal G\), the overlap counts the rectangles through a point, and the shadow is their union:

\[h_{\mathcal G}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal G}\mathbf1_I, \qquad \mathrm{sh}(\mathcal G)=\bigcup_{I\in\mathcal G}I.\]

The maximal function is the supremum of the averages of \(|f|\) over rectangles containing the point. The specification defines these averages using nonnegative integrals, which may take the value infinity. The hypotheses of the maximal estimates ensure integrability on each rectangle. Lean proves that these averages agree with the ordinary real-valued averages used in the proofs.

For countable families, the overlap sum and maximal supremum are initially allowed to be infinite. Under the hypotheses of the respective \(L^p\) maximal and sparse overlap estimates, Lean proves that they are finite almost everywhere. Their real-valued versions therefore represent the same quantities outside a null set, preserving the norms and integrals used in the estimates.

Some intermediate bounds use the exponential minus one. Adding the measure of the shadow gives the full exponential bounds in the statements, with an enlarged constant. This conversion is proved in Lean.

Lean: weak incomparability, equivalent containment condition, sparseness, incomparability, equivalent definitions of sparseness, overlap, shadow, agreement of the two integral formulas, the independently defined maximal function, full exponential bounds, and the countable-family arguments for maximal functions and overlap.

The correspondence between the two grid descriptions preserves all products of grid cubes and all rectangles satisfying the prescribed dyadic side-length relation. See the equalities of rectangle families.

Continuous rectangles, rounding and measurability

The continuous family \(\mathcal R_\phi\) consists of rectangles at arbitrary locations with side lengths \((s_1,\ldots,s_{m-1},\phi(s_1,\ldots,s_{m-1}))\), for all \(s_i>0\). The endpoint theorem assumes that \(\phi\) is positive and coordinatewise nondecreasing; it requires no continuity or differentiability.

To cover these rectangles by dyadic rectangles, the paper uses \(\rho(s)=\lceil\log_2s\rceil+2\) and the corresponding shifted grids. The rounded family contains every side-length tuple obtained in this way. The same first \(m-1\) rounded scales may occur with more than one last scale; the definition retains all of them. The continuous and rounded families described independently in the statements are proved to equal those used in the proofs.

For locally integrable \(f\), the nonnegative integrals defining the averages agree with the real integrals, and hence give the same maximal functions. The paper's shifted grids use \([a,b)\) in each scalar coordinate, while Mathlib's boxes use \((a,b]\). Lean proves the following agreements between the resulting maximal functions and checks measurability:

  • For the countable rounded family, the two conventions give the same maximal function almost everywhere and the same level-set measures.
  • For the continuous family at arbitrary locations, the two conventions give the same maximal function at every point. The proof uses continuity under translation of the local integrals.
  • The continuous maximal function is measurable.

Lean: continuous rectangles, rounding, equality of the continuous families, shifted cubes, equality of the rounded families, agreement of the maximal functions, the endpoint maximal function, rounded level-set measures, continuous-family boundary equality, and continuous measurability.

Sharpness integrals

The sharpness construction gives finite families of Euclidean rectangles and pairwise disjoint subsets establishing their sparseness. For each family, the overlap is bounded and its shadow has finite measure, so the full exponential integral over the shadow is finite. The divergence statement says that these finite values tend to \(+\infty\) along the sequence of families. Lean proves that the ordinary real integral and the nonnegative integral give the same value for each family, and that their formulations of this limiting statement agree. The sharpness statement uses the ordinary real integral.

Lean: the rectangle construction, integrability of the exponential, equality of the integrals, equivalence of the divergence statements, and full exponential sharpness.

Cross-coordinate averages and the projection

Fix a rectangle \(I_0=I_0^1\times\cdots\times I_0^m\). In each coordinate, take the finite collection of dyadic subcubes of \(I_0^i\) down to a common smallest size, including \(I_0^i\) itself. Let \(\mathcal G\) be a family of rectangles formed from these cubes. Fix \(j\) and choose \(K^i\) from these collections for \(i\ne j\). Collect the cubes \(I^j\) from those \(I\in\mathcal G\) for which \(K^i\subset I^i\) whenever \(i\ne j\), and adjoin all the smallest cubes in \(I_0^j\). The maximal cubes of this collection partition \(I_0^j\). A cube arising from several rectangles is included only once. If no rectangle contributes a cube, the partition consists of the smallest cubes.

The cross-coordinate average \(X_{K^{A_j}}\) is the sum of the averaging operators over this partition, acting in coordinate \(j\), where \(A_j=\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus\{j\}\). It preserves measurability.

Writing \(\mathrm{ch}(Q)\) for the dyadic children of \(Q\), the difference operators are \(\Delta_Q=\sum_{R\in\mathrm{ch}(Q)}E_R-E_Q\) and \(\Delta_{K^A}=\prod_{i\in A}\Delta_{K^i}\). For \(f\) constant on the smallest product cubes and extended by zero outside \(I_0\), the projected function \(F\) is obtained by subtracting the product martingale differences indexed by subrectangles of members of \(\mathcal G\), each counted once. Only finitely many of these differences are nonzero. The full grid formula for \(F\) is proved to agree with the finite projection. The formalization proves the paper's identity

\[\Delta_{K^{A_j}}F=X_{K^{A_j}}\Delta_{K^{A_j}}f\]

for all the chosen cubes \(K^i\), including those of the smallest size. This relates differences of \(F\) in \(m-1\) coordinates to an average in the remaining coordinate.

Lean: partitions and their averages, the full grid sum and finite projection, and the identity for the projected function.

Scope of these checks

The definition review covers the main theorems and the supporting results. The examples above highlight the principal constructions. Lebesgue measure, integration, powers and limits use their usual Mathlib definitions.

All six independently stated main results passed Comparator comparison, and their exported proofs and exported dependencies were accepted by Lean’s default kernel, Nanoda and con-ron. Supporting results outside those exports are covered by the ordinary Lean checks described in the verification guide; their presence in the library does not imply separate Nanoda or con-ron verification.
